A decision tool

In-house vs. going solo

An interactive model for the full economics a trainer faces when considering leaving a studio for a personal-training sublease. Designed to surface what the headline hourly rate doesn't.

The single biggest insight this tool exposes: at most in-house studios, a trainer's income isn't 19 personal-training sessions a week. It's a product mix the studio designed: group classes that pay like a senior trainer for one hour of work, partner sessions that double hourly throughput, and solo PT on top. A personal-training sublease gives you a room. It doesn't give you the product mix.

Costs & leakage

Late cancels at an established studio are typically charged to the client and paid to the coach. Solo, that revenue evaporates. Self-employment tax (Social Security + Medicare) is owed in full by the self-employed — the employer paid half of it as a W-2 worker.

The unpaid hours nobody quotes you
Going solo doesn't just mean taking back the programming and scheduling work the studio currently does. It means an entirely new category of work the trainer has never done at scale: marketing, lead generation, sales calls, intake and onboarding, contracts, bookkeeping, quarterly estimated tax filings, insurance and license renewals, equipment maintenance, social content, retention and win-back campaigns, no-show chasing, and being the only person who handles a sick day or a broken cable machine. Realistic estimate: 10–15 hours per week on top of training itself. If group classes are part of the plan, add 3–5 more hours for programming them.
